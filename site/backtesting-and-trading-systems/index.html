Backtesting and Trading Systems

A backtest is an experiment, not a promise. The work is making the experiment honest enough that its result is worth anything at all.

Sixteen lessons that turn "it looks like this would have worked" into evidence you can defend. They start with what a historical simulation can and cannot establish, and with writing a market idea down as rules deterministic enough that the same data always produces the same decision. From there: the dataset and the clock — missing bars, stale prices, a point-in-time universe that still contains the companies that failed, how raw trades become bars, and the signal timing that keeps tomorrow's price out of today's decision. Then the account itself, simulated fill by fill: orders that could realistically have executed, cash and equity and rejected orders, and the commission, spread, slippage and impact deducted at every entry and exit. The last third is measurement and doubt: the trade log and the drawdown, expectancy alongside win rate, a benchmark and the risk taken to beat it, data held back until the system is fixed, walk-forward windows, and the sensitivity, resampling and stress tests that show whether a result survives away from one exact historical path. Lesson 16 writes the whole thing down as a versioned specification. Every lesson is one self-contained interactive page — no accounts, no tracking, nothing to install.

The course

What this course is

One question, taken apart in the order you have to answer it: what exactly are the rules, what data were they tested on, what would the fills and the costs really have been, what did the result measure — and would it survive data the rules never saw?

16 lessons · 6 stages

What it teaches

What a backtest establishes and what it only suggests, and how to state an idea as entry, exit, sizing and invalidation rules that are deterministic (01–02). Then the dataset and the clock: gaps, duplicates, stale values and outliers; a point-in-time universe that keeps the delisted names and adjusts splits without hindsight; how trades become bars and which sessions count; and the signal timing that keeps future information out of a past decision (03–06). Then the simulated account: order behaviour and realistic fills, cash, equity, open positions and rejected orders, and commissions, spread, slippage and market impact charged at every entry and exit (07–09). Then measurement: an auditable trade log, the equity curve and its drawdown; win rate, average win and loss, expectancy, profit factor and sample size; and a benchmark that separates market exposure from independent performance (10–12). Then doubt, applied on purpose: in-sample, validation and out-of-sample splits, walk-forward windows, and sensitivity, resampling, higher costs and stress tests (13–15). Lesson 16 writes the whole system down and reports it.

Who it is for

Anyone who has produced a beautiful equity curve and cannot say why it should be believed. It assumes you can read a chart, and that risk per trade, R-multiples, expectancy and drawdown already mean something to you — course 6 defines them. It assumes nothing about walk-forward analysis, Monte Carlo resampling or point-in-time data. It also suits the reader who has never written a backtest and wants to know what the result of one is worth: the honest answer is that a simulation is evidence about a past sequence, that costs and timing decide most of it, and that a curve fitted to the data it was chosen on is not evidence at all.

How to work through it

In order, 01 to 16, one stage at a time. Each stage is the prerequisite for the next: the rules written in 02 are what the data in 03 to 06 is fed to, the fills in 07 and the costs in 09 are what the equity curve in 10 is made of, the metrics in 11 are meaningless without the sample size that lesson also insists on, and the split in 13 is what makes the walk-forward in 14 and the stress tests in 15 more than a second look at the same fitted result. One lesson per sitting is plenty. Stage 5 is where most readers discover that their best backtest was a search, not a test.

Where the definitions come from

The labs are simplified on purpose, so each lesson closes with an official reading link to a primary source rather than to a vendor selling backtesting software: FINRA's investor explainer stating that historical backtesting is hypothetical and does not predict future performance, its guidance on online trading, and FINRA Rule 2210 on communications with the public, which restricts performance projections and misleading performance claims; CME Group for market data and for what a trading simulator is; and the SEC's investor.gov on understanding fees. Those are definitions from a regulator, a self-regulatory organization and the venue — the course's own synthetic examples are illustrations of them, never a substitute.

Every lesson still works with no network at all. Each page is entirely self-contained, and the reference links are the only things on it that point off the site.

It does not provide live market data, trading signals, profitability claims, personalized investment advice, or an execution system. Data and results scope. All price paths, trades, universes, fills, costs, folds, and Monte Carlo paths in these lessons are deterministic synthetic examples. Historical simulations are treated throughout as hypothetical evidence, never as predictions. The course uses stated conventions — signals are separated from order submission and execution; costs are applied at every entry and exit rather than deducted once from the final result; time-series splits remain chronological; out-of-sample and walk-forward results are reported separately from training results; and robustness testing means nearby parameters, resampled trade sequences, higher costs, and the removal of favourable periods. A simulated result is not a realized result: real outcomes vary by data source, vendor, venue, asset class, session, liquidity, borrow, capacity, and the execution you actually get.

Stage 1 of 6 Lessons 01–02

What a backtest is

Before any of the machinery: what a historical simulation actually establishes, and what it only ever suggests. A backtest is a claim about one recorded sequence under a set of assumptions you chose, so this stage separates the assumptions from the result — and then makes the strategy itself precise enough to test at all. An idea that a person has to interpret produces a different backtest every time it is run; rules that are deterministic produce the same decision from the same data, which is the minimum condition for everything that follows.

Stage 2 of 6 Lessons 03–06

The data and the clock

Most backtests are wrong before a single rule runs, because of what was in the file and when the strategy was allowed to see it. This stage covers both halves. First the dataset: the defects that manufacture signals, and the universe question — a list of today's survivors, adjusted with today's knowledge, quietly guarantees a good result. Then the clock: how raw trades become the bars a rule reads, which sessions are eligible, and the timing discipline that keeps a completed bar's close, a later revision, or a statistic computed over the whole file out of a decision made before any of it existed.

Stage 3 of 6 Lessons 07–09

Simulating the account

A signal is not a trade. This stage builds the machinery between them: an order that only fills at a price the tested sequence could actually have traded, an account that tracks cash, equity, buying power and every open position bar by bar — including the orders it had to reject — and the costs charged on the way in and the way out. Costs are where most promising systems go: commissions, the spread you cross, the slippage you get instead of the price you wanted, and the impact of size the market never offered. They are deducted at every fill here, not subtracted once at the end, because that is the only version that answers what the strategy would have kept.

Stage 4 of 6 Lessons 10–12

Measuring the result

Now there is something to measure, and the question becomes what to measure it with. The trade log is the primary record — every entry, exit, size and cost, reconstructible into the equity curve, and from that curve the drawdown a real account would have had to sit through. Then the summary statistics, plural and on purpose: win rate says nothing without the average win and the average loss beside it, expectancy and profit factor say nothing without the number of trades behind them. And a return is only impressive relative to something — a benchmark and a cash rate, so that exposure to the market is not mistaken for skill at trading it.

Stage 5 of 6 Lessons 13–15

Validation and robustness

A good result on the data you tuned on is not evidence — it is a description of that data. This stage is the part of the course that tries to break the system on purpose. First the split: rules developed on one period, parameters chosen on a second, and a final period left sealed until the system is fixed, in chronological order so the future never trains the past. Then walk-forward, which repeats that discipline through time and stitches the unseen windows into a single forward simulation. Then the stress: nearby parameter values, resampled trade sequences, higher costs, favourable periods removed — because a result that only exists at one exact setting on one exact history is a coincidence with a chart attached.

Interop

Lesson 16 exports a system specification

The last lesson is not a quiz you pass and close. It turns the whole course into one document — what the system claims, what it was tested on, how it was filled and charged, how it was validated, and what it has to achieve to be accepted — and hands that document back to you as a file, so it can outlive the browser tab it was written in.

Format: trading-system-specification-v1

What comes out of it

Lesson 16 takes the identity and version you gave the system, the hypothesis with its market regime and direction, the data section — universe, timeframe, point-in-time handling, corporate actions, timezone — the entry, exit and position-sizing rules, the execution assumptions in basis points for commission, spread and slippage per side, the validation plan with its in-sample, validation and out-of-sample percentages, its walk-forward flag and its benchmark, the acceptance criteria, and the reporting commitments, and exports the whole specification as a JSON document stamped trading-system-specification-v1.

The schema linked beside this is the contract for that shape. It is worth reading if you would rather keep your systems in your own notebook or repository: match the shape and the same fields carry over. All nine top-level members are required, so a specification that omits its validation plan or its acceptance criteria does not validate — those are the two sections a hopeful backtest leaves out first. Note what the shape deliberately does NOT contain: a realized result. The numbers under acceptance — minimumTrades, maximumDrawdownPct, minimumExpectancyR — are thresholds you commit to in advance, not performance the system achieved, so a system that is merely well specified is never mistaken for one that has been shown to work. Deployment, monitoring and live execution are course 8's subject. Everything runs in your browser — nothing is uploaded, and there is no account anywhere in this course.
