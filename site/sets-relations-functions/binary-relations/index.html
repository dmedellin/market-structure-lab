Binary Relations

Any subset of A × B is a relation. Three ways to present one — as pairs, as a matrix, as a directed graph — and the operations of inverse and composition.

Pairs, and what you can do with them

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The definition is deliberately weak: any set of pairs qualifies. The interesting relations are the ones with extra properties, from lesson 7 on.

1

A relation is just a set of pairs

No condition at all. {(1,1), (2,5)} is a relation on {1,2,5}, and so is the empty set.

2

Three presentations, one object

The pair list, the boolean matrix and the directed graph carry identical information. Each makes different properties obvious.

3

Composition is not commutative

S ∘ R follows R then S. Reversing the order generally gives a different relation, and often an empty one.

The material

Relations and their presentations

The definition, three ways to draw it, and two operations.

Definition · Binary relation

A relation from A to B is a subset R ⊆ A × B. A relation on A is a subset of A × A. We write a R b for (a, b) ∈ R.

The definition permits everything. ∅ is a relation (nothing is related), and A × A is a relation (everything is related to everything). Between those extremes there are 2^(n²) relations on an n-element set — 512 on a three-element set, and over half a billion on a five-element one.

Three presentations

  • As pairs. R = {(1,2), (2,3), (3,1)}. Complete and unreadable beyond a handful.
  • As a matrix. An n × n grid with a 1 in row a, column b when a R b. Reflexivity is the diagonal, symmetry is the matrix equalling its transpose.
  • As a directed graph. A dot per element, an arrow from a to b when a R b. Transitivity becomes "every two-step path has a one-step shortcut", which is the reading course 7 uses.

The matrix is what the lab shows, because the four properties of lesson 7 are all visible in it: the diagonal, the transpose, and the presence of both (a,b) and (b,a) for distinct a, b.

Definition · Inverse relation

R⁻¹ = {(b, a) : (a, b) ∈ R} — every pair reversed. As a matrix this is the transpose.

Definition · Composition

For R ⊆ A × B and S ⊆ B × C, S ∘ R = {(a, c) : ∃b ∈ B with (a,b) ∈ R and (b,c) ∈ S}. Read right to left: apply R first, then S.

The right-to-left order is chosen to match function composition, where (g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)). It is a convention and some texts reverse it; what matters is being consistent and saying which you mean.

Example · Composing two relations

On {1,2,3}, let R = {(1,2), (2,3)} and S = {(2,1), (3,3)}. Then S ∘ R = {(1,1), (2,3)}: from 1, R reaches 2 and S takes 2 to 1; from 2, R reaches 3 and S takes 3 to 3. Meanwhile R ∘ S = {(2,2)} — a different relation.

Theorem · Composition and matrices

If M_R and M_S are the boolean matrices of R and S, then the matrix of S ∘ R is the boolean product M_R M_S, where multiplication is ∧ and addition is ∨.

That is the same computation as counting walks of length 2 in course 7 lesson 3, with ∨ in place of +. Composition of relations and paths in a directed graph are the same idea.

Method

Reading a relation

Choose the presentation that makes your question easy.

1

Write it as pairs when it is small

Nothing is clearer than the list for four or five pairs, and the list is what the definition actually is.

2

Use the matrix to check properties

Reflexive is a full diagonal; symmetric is equal to its transpose. Both are one glance in matrix form and tedious in list form.

3

Use the digraph to trace paths

Transitivity and the transitive closure are statements about paths, and arrows make paths visible.

4

Compose right to left

S ∘ R means R first. Pick each a, follow R to every b, then S to every c, and collect the pairs (a, c).

Composing with itself

On {1,2,3,4}, let R = {(1,2), (2,3), (3,4)} — the successor relation.

R ∘ R (two steps): 1 → 2 → 3 gives (1, 3) 2 → 3 → 4 gives (2, 4) 3 → 4 → ? 4 has no outgoing pair — nothing R ∘ R = {(1,3), (2,4)} R ∘ R ∘ R (three steps): {(1,4)} R⁴ = ∅ no path of length 4 exists in a 4-element chain R ∪ R² ∪ R³ = {(1,2),(2,3),(3,4),(1,3),(2,4),(1,4)} = the relation "a < b", which is the transitive closure of R.

The last line is lesson 7's transitive closure computed by hand: the union of all powers of R is exactly the pairs joined by a path of any length. Warshall's algorithm in the lab does this without enumerating the powers.

Common mistakes

1
Composing in the wrong orderS ∘ R applies R first. Getting this backwards produces a different relation, usually a different size, and the error is silent.
2
Assuming a relation must be meaningfulAny set of pairs qualifies. Most relations have no description shorter than their pair list, which is why properties rather than names are what matter.
3
Confusing R⁻¹ with a complementThe inverse reverses each pair; the complement takes the pairs not in R. They are unrelated operations.

Completion standard

✓
Finish when you can move between all three presentations.

Take R = {(1,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,1)} on {1,2,3}, write its matrix, sketch its digraph, and compute R ∘ R in whichever presentation is easiest. Then check the answer in a second presentation.

Note: Relations need not be on one set: R ⊆ A × B with A ≠ B is a bipartite relation, and lesson 10's functions are exactly the relations from A to B in which every element of A appears exactly once as a first coordinate.
