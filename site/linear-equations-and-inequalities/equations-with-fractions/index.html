Equations with Fractions

Multiplying both sides by a common denominator turns an equation full of fractions into one with none. The move is the multiplication property with a convenient constant, so it changes nothing about the answer — provided the multiplier reaches every term on both sides.

One multiplication, applied everywhere

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Nothing here is a new rule about fractions. It is one application of the multiplication property, chosen so that the arithmetic afterwards is easy.

1

Clearing denominators is the multiplication property in disguise

Multiplying both sides by a nonzero number produces an equivalent equation. The LCD is just a well-chosen value of that number, and the equation you get has exactly the same solutions as the one you started with.

2

The multiplier reaches every term on both sides

Including the terms that have no denominator. Multiplying x/2 + x/3 = 5 by 6 and leaving the 5 alone gives 3x + 2x = 5, an equation about something else entirely.

3

The least common denominator is a convenience, not a requirement

Any common multiple of the denominators clears them. 12 works on x/2 and x/3 as well as 6 does; it merely doubles every number in the rest of the problem. Choosing the least one is a habit, not a law.

The material

Removing the denominators

Finding the multiplier, applying it to every term, and the two places the brackets matter.

Definition · Least common denominator

The least common denominator of a set of fractions is the smallest positive number that every denominator divides exactly — the least common multiple of the denominators.

For 2 and 3 it is 6. For 4 and 6 it is 12, not 24: 24 is the product, and the product is a common multiple but not usually the least one.

x/2 + x/3 = 5 × 6 both sides 6·(x/2) + 6·(x/3) = 6·5 3x + 2x = 30 5x = 30 x = 6 check 6/2 + 6/3 = 3 + 2 = 5

Using 12 instead gives 6x + 4x = 60, so 10x = 60 and x = 6. The same answer, reached through larger numbers. That is the only penalty for not using the least common denominator.

A numerator with more than one term

(x + 1)/3 is the whole of x + 1 divided by 3. When the multiplier arrives, the bracket has to stay:

(x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/4 = 1 LCD of 3 and 4 is 12 × 12 4(x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 12 expand 4x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 12 collect x + 10 = 12 x = 2 check (2 + 1)/3 − (2 − 2)/4 = 1 − 0 = 1

Two things happen in the expansion and both are worth naming. 12 · (x + 1)/3 is 4(x + 1), which is 4x + 4 — not 4x + 1. And −3(x − 2) is −3x + 6, because the minus reaches both terms inside the bracket.

Decimals are fractions

0.2 is 1/5, 1.5 is 3/2 and 3.1 is 31/10. Multiplying by a power of ten clears them all at once: use 10 for one decimal place, 100 for two, and so on for the longest one present.

0.2x + 1.5 = 3.1 × 10 2x + 15 = 31 2x = 16 x = 8 check 0.2·8 + 1.5 = 1.6 + 1.5 = 3.1

What clearing does not change

The multiplier here is a fixed nonzero number, so the move is reversible: divide by it and you are back where you started. The solution set therefore cannot change, and no candidate needs to be thrown out afterwards.

That guarantee depends on the multiplier being a number. When a denominator contains the variable — 3/(x − 2) — the multiplier is zero for one value of x, the move is not reversible there, and a candidate can appear that solves nothing. Lesson 7 meets the first case of that, and course 5 treats it properly.

Example · When the fractions cancel completely

x/6 + x/3 = x/2, multiplied through by 6, becomes x + 2x = 3x, that is 3x = 3x. Every x cancels and 0 = 0 is left. The two sides were the same expression written differently — x/6 + x/3 = x/6 + 2x/6 = 3x/6 = x/2 — so every number is a solution. Lesson 6 names this outcome.

Method

Clearing the denominators

Find the multiplier first, then use it on everything without exception.

1

List the denominators, converting any decimals

0.2 counts as 1/5. Write the denominators down rather than holding them in your head; the list is what the next step works from.

2

Take the least common multiple of that list

That is the LCD. Any common multiple works, so if the least one is not obvious, the product of the denominators is a safe fallback that costs only larger arithmetic.

3

Multiply every term on both sides by it, keeping brackets on multi-term numerators

Count the terms first and count them again afterwards. 12 · (x + 1)/3 is 4(x + 1); the bracket is not optional, and neither is multiplying the terms that had no denominator.

4

Solve the fraction-free equation, then check in the original

The check goes back into the equation with the fractions in it. That is the one that was asked, and it is the only version that can confirm the clearing step itself.

Two clearings, with the traps marked

The first has multi-term numerators and a subtraction; the second is decimals.

A. (x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/4 = 1 denominators 3, 4 LCD = 12 × 12 term by term: 12 · (x + 1)/3 = 4(x + 1) <-- bracket kept 12 · (x − 2)/4 = 3(x − 2) 12 · 1 = 12 <-- the right side too 4(x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 12 4x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 12 <-- −3(x − 2) = −3x + 6 x + 10 = 12 x = 2 check (2 + 1)/3 − (2 − 2)/4 = 1 − 0 = 1 agrees B. 0.2x + 1.5 = 3.1 one decimal place, so multiply by 10 2x + 15 = 31 2x = 16 x = 8 check 0.2·8 + 1.5 = 1.6 + 1.5 = 3.1 agrees

Three terms went into the multiplication in A and three came out. That count is the fastest guard against the standard error, which is multiplying the fractions and leaving the 1 on the right untouched.

The sign on −3(x − 2) is the second trap. It produces +6, and writing −6 instead gives x = 14 — an answer that survives every later step and fails the check.

Common mistakes

1
Multiplying only the terms that have denominatorsIn x/2 + x/3 = 5 the 5 gets multiplied too. Leaving it alone produces 5x = 5 and the answer 1, which fails the check on the very first substitution.
2
Dropping the bracket on a numerator with two terms12 · (x + 1)/3 is 4(x + 1) = 4x + 4, not 4x + 1. The fraction bar acts as a bracket, and it disappears at exactly the moment the bracket becomes necessary.
3
Losing the sign when a fraction is subtracted−3(x − 2) is −3x + 6. The minus in front of the fraction belongs to the whole numerator, so it reaches both terms once the denominator has gone.

Completion standard

✓
Finish when you can clear any set of denominators in one multiplication and account for every term.

Given an equation with fractions or decimals you should name the denominators, state the LCD, multiply every term including the ones on the right, keep brackets around multi-term numerators, and check in the original. The term count before and after should be the first thing you verify.

Note: Lesson 5 rearranges formulas, where the fractions are made of letters rather than numbers and exactly the same multiplication clears them. The condition that the multiplier be nonzero, which is free here, becomes the main thing to state there.
