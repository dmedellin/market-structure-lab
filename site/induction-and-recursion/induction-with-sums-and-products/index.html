Induction with Sums and Products

Sums of integers, squares, cubes, geometric series and telescoping sums. Every proof has the same shape, and recognising it makes these routine.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

These proofs are mechanical once the pattern is visible: peel, substitute, factor.

1

Peel off the last term

Σ_{i≤k+1} f(i) = (Σ_{i≤k} f(i)) + f(k+1). This split is what lets the hypothesis apply to the first part.

2

Substitute the hypothesis

Replace the sum to k by its closed form. From here it is algebra with no sums in it.

3

Factor toward the target

Write the claim at k+1 first, so you know what you are aiming at. Working without a target is how these proofs stall.

The material

The standard sums

Notation, then the proofs, then a route that avoids induction entirely.

Definition · Summation notation

Σ_{i=1}^{n} f(i) means f(1) + f(2) + ⋯ + f(n). The index i is bound; the sum is empty and equal to 0 when the upper limit is below the lower. Products use Π and an empty product is 1.

Theorem · Sum of squares

For n ≥ 1, 1² + 2² + ⋯ + n² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6.

Base. At n = 1: left is 1, right is 1 · 2 · 3 / 6 = 1.

Step. Assume the identity at k. Then

Σ_{i≤k+1} i² = k(k+1)(2k+1)/6 + (k+1)² by the hypothesis = (k+1)[k(2k+1) + 6(k+1)]/6 = (k+1)(2k² + 7k + 6)/6 = (k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)/6,

which is the claim at k+1, since 2(k+1)+1 = 2k+3.

□

The factoring step 2k² + 7k + 6 = (k+2)(2k+3) is the only place cleverness is required, and it is found by knowing the target: the claim at k+1 must contain (k+2), so (k+2) is what to look for.

Theorem · Geometric series

For r ≠ 1 and n ≥ 0, a + ar + ar² + ⋯ + arⁿ = a(rⁿ⁺¹ − 1)/(r − 1).

Base. At n = 0 the left is a and the right is a(r − 1)/(r − 1) = a.

Step. Assume the formula at k. Adding the next term,

a(r^{k+1} − 1)/(r − 1) + ar^{k+1} = [a(r^{k+1} − 1) + ar^{k+1}(r − 1)]/(r − 1) = a(r^{k+2} − 1)/(r − 1),

which is the claim at k+1.

□

The condition r ≠ 1 is not decoration: at r = 1 the denominator vanishes and the sum is simply a(n+1). Every hypothesis in a theorem statement is doing work somewhere, and finding where is a good habit.

Telescoping, and proofs without induction

Some sums collapse without any induction at all. If f(i) = g(i) − g(i−1), then Σ_{i=1}^{n} f(i) = g(n) − g(0), because every intermediate term appears once positively and once negatively.

Example · A telescoping sum

Σ_{i=1}^{n} 1/(i(i+1)). Since 1/(i(i+1)) = 1/i − 1/(i+1), the sum is (1 − 1/2) + (1/2 − 1/3) + ⋯ + (1/n − 1/(n+1)) = 1 − 1/(n+1) = n/(n+1). No induction needed, though induction would work.

Gauss's pairing argument for Σ i is another such route: add the sum to its own reversal, obtaining n copies of n+1, hence n(n+1)/2. Induction verifies a formula you already have; arguments like these produce one.

Method

Proving a sum identity

Write the target first; the algebra then has somewhere to go.

1

Write the claim at k+1 before starting

Substituting k+1 into the formula tells you what the algebra must produce. Working forward blindly is how these stall.

2

Peel the last term off the sum

Σ_{i≤k+1} = (Σ_{i≤k}) + f(k+1). This is the only structural move.

3

Substitute the hypothesis and factor

Take out the common factor — usually (k+1) — and simplify what remains toward the target.

4

Check the base case at the right place

n = 0 and n = 1 are different starting points and give different base cases. Use whichever the statement claims.

Prove Σ_{i=1}^{n} i·i! = (n+1)! − 1

A less familiar identity, same shape of proof.

BASE n = 1: left = 1·1! = 1, right = 2! − 1 = 1. ✓ STEP Assume Σ_{i≤k} i·i! = (k+1)! − 1. Σ_{i≤k+1} i·i! = [(k+1)! − 1] + (k+1)(k+1)! = (k+1)!·[1 + (k+1)] − 1 = (k+1)!·(k+2) − 1 = (k+2)! − 1 ← claim at k+1 CONCLUDE Holds for all n ≥ 1. ∎

The move that makes it work is factoring (k+1)! out of both terms, which is available because (k+1)(k+1)! shares it. Recognising (k+1)!(k+2) = (k+2)! is the same kind of target-driven step as before.

Common mistakes

1
Not writing the target before doing the algebraThe claim at k+1 tells you which factorisation to aim for. Without it the step becomes trial and error.
2
Peeling the wrong termThe sum to k+1 splits as the sum to k plus f(k+1). Splitting off the first term instead leaves a sum the hypothesis does not describe.
3
Using the wrong base caseA formula claimed for n ≥ 0 needs n = 0 checked. Starting at 1 leaves the claim unproved exactly where it is most likely to be false.

Completion standard

✓
Finish when these proofs are mechanical.

Prove Σ_{i=1}^{n} (2i − 1)² = n(2n−1)(2n+1)/3 — the sum of the squares of the odd numbers. Same three moves, slightly heavier algebra, and no new idea required.

Note: Sums of kth powers have closed forms for every k, given by Faulhaber's formula, and each is a polynomial of degree k+1 in n. Induction verifies any one of them; producing them requires a different technique.
