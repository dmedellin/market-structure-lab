Trading Risk Management

You do not control whether a trade works. You control what it costs when it does not.

Sixteen lessons that start with the only quantity in trading you set yourself — the loss you planned before you entered — and follow it everywhere it goes. The risk budget divides it across trades, open positions, the day and the week. The stop puts it where the idea is actually wrong, and position size is what makes that distance affordable rather than the other way round. From there it becomes arithmetic you can check: outcomes in R, expectancy with costs in it, the drawdown a normal losing streak produces, and the probability of never coming back from one. The last third is everything that moves the planned loss without asking — volatility, gaps and thin liquidity, leverage, positions that turn out to be the same position, and options, where the risk is not always bounded — ending in a written risk plan precise enough to export. Every lesson is one self-contained interactive page — no accounts, no tracking, nothing to install.

The course

What this course is

One question, taken apart in the order you have to answer it: how much can this trade cost, how much can this week cost, and what would have to happen for the account to stop being able to trade at all.

16 lessons · 6 stages

What it teaches

Which risks you control and which you only absorb, and how a planned loss is stated before entry; then the account risk budget across the trade, the open book, the day and the week (01–02). The single trade next: fixed-percentage risk and what it does to account decay, the stop that sits where the thesis is invalid, and the size that makes that distance affordable (03–05). Then the arithmetic of a sequence — R-multiples, expectancy with costs in it, drawdown and its recovery requirement, and risk of ruin (06–09). Then what moves the plan without asking: volatility and ATR, gaps, slippage and thin liquidity, leverage and margin (10–12), correlated and concentrated exposure, and options risk including assignment and undefined-risk positions (13–14). Lesson 15 sets the hard daily and weekly limits, and lesson 16 turns all of it into one testable specification.

Who it is for

Anyone who can find a setup and cannot say what it is allowed to cost. It assumes you can read a chart, place a stop and tell a call from a put; it assumes nothing about expectancy, drawdown mathematics, margin mechanics or correlation. It also suits the reader who has been sizing positions by round numbers of shares and has quietly noticed that a wide stop and a narrow stop then risk wildly different amounts — the course is explicit that size is an output, computed from the risk you allowed and the distance to invalidation, and never an input you pick first.

How to work through it

In order, 01 to 16, one stage at a time. Each stage is the prerequisite for the next: the budget in 02 is what risk per trade in 03 spends, the stop in 04 is the distance position size in 05 divides by, R in 06 is the unit expectancy in 07 is measured in, and the drawdown in 08 is what makes risk of ruin in 09 a number rather than a worry. Stage 4 is where most readers find out that the loss they planned and the loss they took are two different quantities. The plan in 16 asks for account limits, sizing, execution constraints, portfolio exposure, options rules and review triggers — one from every stage before it — so it is best attempted last, not skipped to.

Where the definitions come from

The labs are simplified on purpose, so each lesson closes with an official reading link to a primary source rather than to a vendor selling a risk calculator: CME Group's education material for trade and risk management, contract multipliers and controlling risk; FINRA for risk tolerance, brokerage and margin accounts, day-trading requirements, diversification and options; the SEC's investor.gov for order types, asset allocation and its bulletins on volatile markets; and OCC's OptionsEducation.org for exercise and assignment. Those are definitions from the exchange, the regulator and the clearing house — the course's own synthetic examples are illustrations of them, never a substitute.

Every lesson still works with no network at all. Each page is entirely self-contained, and the reference links are the only things on it that point off the site.

Curriculum

The sixteen lessons, in order

Six stages, grouped the way the sequence itself groups: the foundations and the budget the whole course spends, the three lessons that size one trade, the arithmetic of a sequence of them, the market conditions that move the planned loss, the exposure that hides across positions and inside options, and the hard limits and written plan that turn all of it into a rule. Each lesson is a single self-contained page of interactive charts, worked explanations, and practice. Start at 01 and work down.

Educational use only — nothing here is trading advice. This course teaches trade, account, execution, leverage, portfolio, and options risk controls. It does not provide live market data, personalized risk limits, trading signals, execution access, profitability claims, or investment advice. Real outcomes depend on instrument, venue, broker, liquidity, taxes, fees, margin rules, and market conditions. Historical strategy validation is reserved for course 7, and automation and live workflow design for course 8.

Data and calculation scope. All charts, trade sequences, Monte Carlo paths, option payoffs, and portfolio shocks in these lessons are deterministic synthetic examples. The course uses stated conventions — fixed-percentage risk is applied to current equity, so dollar risk decreases during drawdown; recovery gain is calculated as 1 / (1 - drawdown) - 1; position size is rounded down after entry-to-stop distance, multiplier, and slippage reserve are included; expectancy is win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss − average cost; the risk-of-ruin output is a simplified Monte Carlo estimate under user-selected assumptions and is not an exact forecast; leverage examples use user-selected maintenance requirements for education, and actual broker and product requirements vary; and options examples use standard 100-share equity-option multipliers with simplified payoff and sensitivity estimates.

Foundations and the risk budget

The two lessons everything else spends. Then the budget that loss comes out of — one figure divided across the individual trade, everything open at once, the session and the week, so that no single decision can quietly commit the whole account.

Stage 3 of 6 Lessons 06–09

Outcomes across a sequence

One trade tells you almost nothing; a hundred of them tell you what you actually have. This stage converts results into a unit that survives changing account size and instrument — the initial risk, R — then builds the four numbers that describe a sequence: expectancy with costs inside it, the drawdown a perfectly normal losing streak produces, the gain required to undo that drawdown, and the probability that a run of them ends the account rather than dents it.

Stage 4 of 6 Lessons 10–12

Market and execution reality

Everything so far assumed the loss you planned is the loss you take. This stage removes that assumption. Volatility changes what a sensible stop distance even is, so the same account risk buys a different position from one week to the next. Gaps, widening spreads and thin books fill you somewhere other than your stop. And leverage decides how much of the account a given price move reaches — buying power tells you what you are permitted to hold, never what you can afford to lose.

Stage 5 of 6 Lessons 13–14

Exposure beyond one position

Risk that was counted correctly one trade at a time can still be counted wrong in aggregate. Five positions that share a sector, a market or a factor are one position wearing five tickets, and stress is exactly when they admit it. Options add a second way for the arithmetic to break: premium is not the only thing at stake, expiration and assignment create exposure you did not choose, and some structures have no floor under the loss at all.

Stage 6 of 6 Lessons 15–16

Limits and the written plan

Every control in this course is a decision made in advance, and the last two lessons are where advance becomes binding. Hard session and weekly limits are the only ones that hold after a bad morning, because they are the ones you are not entitled to re-evaluate while losing. Then the whole course collapses into a single written specification — account limits, sizing, execution constraints, portfolio exposure, options rules and review triggers — precise enough that another person, or a program, could apply it the same way you would.

Interop

Lesson 16 exports a risk plan

The last lesson is not a quiz you pass and close. It turns every limit you set into explicit fields, checks them against each other, and then hands the whole plan back to you as a file, so it can outlive the browser tab it was written in.

Format: trading-risk-plan-v1

What comes out of it

Lesson 16 takes the account limits you chose — reference equity, risk per trade, maximum open risk and maximum gross leverage — the daily, weekly and maximum-drawdown loss limits, the portfolio constraints on concurrent, correlated and single-sector exposure, the execution constraints for slippage reserve and scheduled events, the options rules covering undefined risk, closing short options before expiration and assignment buying power, and the actions you decided to take when a limit is reached, and exports the whole specification as a JSON document stamped trading-risk-plan-v1.

The schema linked beside this is the contract for that shape. It is worth reading if you would rather keep your limits in your own notebook or script: match the shape and the same fields carry over. Note what the export deliberately does NOT contain: any measure of what the plan earned. There is no performance object anywhere in the shape, and every percentage in it is a constraint you set, not a result — so a plan that is merely well specified is never mistaken for one that has been shown to work. Validation is course 7's subject. Everything runs in your browser — nothing is uploaded, and there is no account anywhere in this course.
