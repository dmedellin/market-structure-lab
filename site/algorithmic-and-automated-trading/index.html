Algorithmic and Automated Trading

Automation changes how a decision reaches the market. It does not decide whether the decision was worth making, and it adds a second set of things that can go wrong: the system itself.

Sixteen lessons on building the thing that runs after the strategy is written — and on the controls that make running it defensible. They start by separating strategy rules, signal generation, risk checks, order submission and execution into parts that can fail independently, then choosing how much authority to delegate: alert, approval, or automatic. From there the machinery: the services and boundaries a decision travels through; market data received, validated, deduplicated and normalized; exchange calendars, session boundaries and deterministic schedulers instead of the assumption that every minute is tradable; a signal engine whose state transitions are repeatable; and a risk engine that owns cash, exposure and limits before an order exists. Then the outside world: a broker adapter where every response is asynchronous state, order types and fill quality measured against the decision price, and a full run on current data with no capital at risk. The last third is what separates a script from a system — duplicate-safe commands, bounded retries and reconciliation after a timeout; metrics, traces and an immutable decision log; scoped credentials, separated environments and a kill switch that stops new trading immediately; reproducible artifacts, gradual rollout and rollback. Lesson 15 puts AI where it belongs: research, extraction, classification and proposals, with risk and execution authority left deterministic. Lesson 16 writes the whole system down as one versioned specification. Every lesson is one self-contained interactive page — no accounts, no broker connection, nothing to install.

The course

What this course is

One question, taken apart in the order you have to answer it: what are the parts, where does the data come from and when is it valid, who decides, who is allowed to send an order — and how do you know what the system did, and stop it when you need to?

16 lessons · 5 stages

What it teaches

How a trading system is split into parts that can be reasoned about separately — strategy rules, signal generation, risk checks, order submission and execution — and how much authority to hand each level of automation (01–02). Then the inputs: historical and streaming data validated, deduplicated and normalized into one model, with late events handled rather than assumed away; and the clock — exchange calendars, time zones, session boundaries, event windows and schedulers that behave the same way twice (03–04). Then the decision itself: a signal engine with explicit state, which is what stops a system repeating, contradicting or acting on a stale signal; and a risk engine that holds cash, positions, exposure, open orders and limits, and is consulted before an order exists (05–06). Then the outside world: a broker adapter that submits, queries, cancels and reconciles while treating every status as asynchronous; order type selection, open-order management and fill quality measured against the decision price; and the whole system run on current data with no capital at risk (07–09). Then the controls: scanning and ranking with deduplicated alerts and decisions that expire; duplicate-safe commands, bounded retries, durable state and reconciliation after a timeout; metrics and traces and an immutable audit trail; scoped credentials, separated environments, rotation and a kill switch; reproducible artifacts, gradual rollout and rollback (10–14). Lesson 15 covers AI-assisted and agentic workflows, and lesson 16 turns all of it into one versioned specification.

Who it is for

Anyone who has a rule set they trust and no idea what it takes to let it run unattended. It assumes you can read a chart, and that risk per trade, position sizing, expectancy and out-of-sample validation already mean something to you — courses 6 and 7 define them. It assumes nothing about distributed systems: idempotency, reconciliation, traces, secret rotation and rollback are introduced here, from the trading problem they solve rather than from the general engineering literature. It also suits the reader who has decided NOT to automate, and wants to know what they would be signing up for: most of this course is about failure — a timeout with unknown state, a duplicate order, a stale price, a credential with more permission than it needs — and none of that goes away because the strategy is good.

How to work through it

In order, 01 to 16, one stage at a time. Each stage is the prerequisite for the next: the boundaries drawn in 02 are what the data in 03 and the clock in 04 are delivered across, the state in 05 and the limits in 06 are what the broker calls in 07 and 08 are allowed to act on, and the reliability, observability and security work in 11 to 13 only makes sense once you have seen an order's status arrive out of order. One lesson per sitting is plenty. Stage 3 is where most readers discover that the hard part was never the strategy: it is what the system does when a response never comes back.

Where the definitions come from

The labs are simplified on purpose, so lessons close with an official reading link to a primary source rather than to a vendor selling an execution platform: FINRA's Regulatory Notice 15-09 on supervision, testing, controls and documentation for algorithmic trading strategies, and the SEC's own summary of Rule 15c3-5, the market-access rule that requires pre-trade financial and regulatory risk controls. Where the subject is engineering rather than regulation, the source is the project that defines the thing: OpenTelemetry on traces, metrics and logs; Kubernetes on liveness and readiness probes; Docker on build practice; the OWASP Secrets Management Cheat Sheet on storing and rotating credentials; and a broker's own published API documentation for paper trading, streaming market data and working with orders — cited as an example of what such an API looks like, not as a recommendation of that broker.

Every lesson still works with no network at all. Each page is entirely self-contained, and the reference links are the only things on it that point off the site.

Curriculum

The sixteen lessons, in order

Five stages, grouped the way the system itself is built: the strategy encoded as parts, data and state; the execution machinery that turns a decision into an order; the testing and the risk controls that decide whether it may run unattended; the deployment, security and monitoring that keep it accountable while it does; and the finish — where AI fits, and what has to be true before anything is switched on. Each lesson is a single self-contained page of interactive charts, worked explanations, and practice. Start at 01 and work down.

Start at lesson 01

Educational use only — nothing here is trading advice. This course does not connect to a broker, ingest live market data, place orders, provide trading signals, or claim that any automated strategy will be profitable. It teaches how such a system is structured and controlled; it is not one, and no page here can submit an order.

Data and results scope. All market paths, events, orders, failures, and portfolio states in these lessons are deterministic synthetic examples. The course uses stated conventions — market data, strategy, risk, order and broker responsibilities stay separate; a network timeout represents unknown state until reconciliation; automatic execution is treated as the final level of automation rather than the starting point; AI components produce advisory proposals while deterministic services retain risk and execution authority; and deployment begins with paper, shadow and limited-scope rollout. Every lesson also separates what the market is doing — buyers accepting higher prices, sellers accepting lower ones, price balanced, volatility expanding — from what the system is doing, because latency, a data gap, a risk rejection or an unknown order state is a fact about your software and not about the market.

Stage 1 of 5 Lessons 01–05

Encoding the strategy

Before anything runs: what the parts of a trading system are, and how much authority each level of automation actually takes. A strategy that lives in your head produces a different trade every time; a strategy encoded as separate rules, signals, risk checks and orders produces the same decision from the same events — which is the minimum condition for everything that follows. This stage draws the boundaries, then fills them in: the data model events arrive as, the calendar and scheduler that say when a decision is even allowed, and the signal engine whose state is explicit enough that a repeat or a stale value cannot slip through as a new trade.

Stage 2 of 5 Lessons 06–08

Execution and infrastructure

The part that touches money. A risk engine that owns cash, positions, exposure, open orders and limits comes first, because it has to be consulted before an order exists rather than after one is rejected. Then the broker adapter, where the comfortable assumption breaks: a submitted order is not a filled order, a response is not a state, and a status update can arrive out of order or not at all. Then the execution decisions themselves — which order type, how long to leave it working, how much of the available liquidity to take, and what the fill actually cost against the price the decision was made at.

Stage 3 of 5 Lessons 09–11

Testing and risk controls

Whether the system may be trusted to run, and what happens when it is wrong. Paper trading runs the whole thing on current data with no capital at risk, which is the first place backtest assumptions meet real signal timing, real latency and real fills. Scanners, alerts and an explicit human approval step are the automation level below automatic, and they have failure modes of their own: duplicated alerts, a thesis the reader cannot reconstruct, a decision acted on an hour after it stopped being true. Then the uncomfortable one — a network that times out leaves you not knowing whether an order exists, and only duplicate-safe commands, durable state and reconciliation get you back to certainty.

Stage 4 of 5 Lessons 12–14

Deployment and monitoring

Running it accountably. Observability first, because a system you cannot measure is a system you are guessing about: data freshness, decision latency, broker latency, error rate, positions and risk, end-to-end traces, and an immutable record of why each decision was made. Then the credentials and the stop: scoped permissions, separated environments, rotation, audited access, and a mechanism that halts new trading immediately and does not depend on the thing it is stopping. Then getting changes into production without changing trading state by accident — reproducible artifacts, configuration separated from code, gradual rollout, and a rollback you have actually tried.

Interop

Lesson 16 exports a system specification

The last lesson of the last course is not a quiz you pass and close. It turns the whole course into one document — what the system trades, where its data comes from, what risk it may take, which broker it speaks to, how it was validated, how it is operated, and what AI is permitted to do — and hands that document back to you as a file, so it can outlive the browser tab it was written in.

Format: automated-trading-system-v1

What comes out of it

Lesson 16 takes the identity and version you gave the system, the strategy section — type, timeframe, direction, and the automation level, which is one of alert, approval or automatic — the market-data section with its mode, its maximum acceptable age in seconds and whether it is normalized, the risk section with its per-trade and gross-exposure percentages, whether risk is evaluated independently and whether a kill switch exists, the order section covering idempotency, reconciliation and the broker adapter, the validation section with its paper-trading days and shadow-mode flag, the operations section with observability, security scope, rollout percentage and rollback, and the AI section, and exports the whole thing as a JSON document stamped automated-trading-system-v1.

The schema linked beside this is the contract for that shape. It is worth reading if you would rather keep your systems in your own notebook or repository: match the shape and the same fields carry over. All nine top-level members are required, so a specification that omits its risk section or its validation plan does not validate — those are the two a hurried system leaves out first. Note what the shape deliberately does contain: ai.mode and ai.directExecution are separate fields, because "an AI is involved" and "an AI can place an order" are different claims and the specification is not allowed to blur them. Note also what it does NOT contain: any realized or expected return. A specification records what a system is permitted to do and what had to be true before it was allowed to run — never what it earned. Everything runs in your browser: nothing is uploaded, no broker is contacted, and there is no account anywhere in this course.

Educational course. This course is educational. It does not connect to a broker, ingest live market data, place orders, provide trading signals, or claim that any automated strategy will be profitable, and no page in it can submit an order. All market paths, events, orders, failures and portfolio states in these lessons are deterministic synthetic examples. Automating a strategy does not reduce its risk: it removes the pause in which a person would have noticed a stale price, a duplicated order, a wrong size or a broken assumption, so an automated system can lose money faster and more consistently than a manual one. Real outcomes vary by data source, vendor, venue, asset class, session, liquidity, latency, broker behaviour and the execution you actually get. Confirm the data, the risk limits, the broker's rules and your own obligations before running anything against a live account.
