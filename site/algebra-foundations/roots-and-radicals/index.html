Roots and Radicals

A radical sign names one number, not two, and the choice of which one is a convention worth stating plainly. Whether that number exists at all depends on the sign of the radicand and on whether the index is even or odd.

One symbol, one value

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Most of the difficulty here is notation rather than arithmetic: what the symbol denotes, and when it denotes nothing.

1

A radical sign names exactly one number

The equation x² = 9 has two solutions, 3 and −3. The expression √9 is a single number, and by convention it is the non-negative one. Confusing the equation with the symbol is the most common error in the topic, and it is not an algebra error.

2

Odd roots always exist; even roots need a non-negative radicand

An even power of a real number is never negative, so nothing real squares to −4 and √(−4) has no value on this course. Odd powers keep the sign of the base, so every real has exactly one real cube root, negatives included.

3

Simplifying means removing complete groups

Factor the radicand into primes. For a square root, each pair of equal primes leaves one factor outside; for an n-th root, each group of n does. What is left under the sign is whatever could not form a full group.

The material

What the symbol denotes, and when it denotes nothing

The principal square root, the general n-th root, why the even case needs absolute value, and how a radical is reduced.

Definition · Principal square root

For x ≥ 0, √x is the unique number r with r ≥ 0 and r² = x.

√0 = 0. For x < 0 there is no real r with r² = x, because the square of any real number is ≥ 0. On this course such a radical has no value and is said to have none; course 6 introduces i and gives it one.

So √9 = 3, and √9 = ±3 is wrong as written. The ± belongs to solving an equation: x² = 9 gives x = ±3, and the ± is put there explicitly because √ alone would only have supplied the positive root. That is also why the quadratic formula in course 6 carries a ± in front of its radical.

Definition · n-th root

For an integer n ≥ 2, an n-th root of x is a number r with rⁿ = x. The n is called the index.

When n is odd, every real x has exactly one real n-th root and it has the same sign as x. When n is even and x > 0 there are two, one positive and one negative, and the radical denotes the positive one; when n is even and x < 0 there are none.

the 3rd root of 8 = 2 2³ = 8 the 3rd root of −8 = −2 (−2)³ = −8 the 4th root of 16 = 2 2⁴ = 16 (−2)⁴ = 16 as well; the radical names 2 the 4th root of −16 = no real value

Why the even case needs absolute value

√(x²) is required to be non-negative, by the definition. At x = −5, x² is 25 and √25 is 5, which is −x rather than x. So the cancellation people write as √(x²) = x is false for every negative x:

x = 5: √(5²) = √25 = 5 = |5| x = −5: √((−5)²) = √25 = 5 = |−5|

The correct statement is √(x²) = |x|, which is lesson 4's two-case definition arriving from a different direction. For an odd index no absolute value is needed: the cube root of x³ is x for every real x, because a cube keeps the sign.

Simplifying a radical

Theorem · Product and quotient rules

For a ≥ 0 and b ≥ 0: √(ab) = √a · √b. For a ≥ 0 and b > 0: √(a/b) = √a / √b.

Both rules hold for an n-th root as well. For odd n no sign restriction is needed, since every real has exactly one real odd root; for even n the radicands must be non-negative, for the same reason they must be here.

There is no corresponding rule for a sum. √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, while √9 + √16 = 7. The radical sign brackets everything under it, exactly as lesson 3 said.

To simplify, factor the radicand into primes and take out each prime in complete groups of the index — pairs for a square root, triples for a cube root:

√72 = √(2³ · 3²) = √(2² · 3² · 2) = 2 · 3 · √2 = 6√2 √30 = √(2 · 3 · 5) already simplest: no prime repeats the 3rd root of 54 = the 3rd root of (3³ · 2) = 3 · (3rd root of 2)

Fractions come apart the same way: √(18/25) = √18 / √25 = 3√2 / 5. When the denominator is not a perfect square the root stays below the bar — √(1/2) = 1/√2 — and clearing it is rationalising, which course 5 covers.

Example · Which of these are real

√16 = 4. √(−16) has no real value. The 3rd root of −27 is −3, because (−3)³ = −27. The 4th root of 81 is 3, because 3⁴ = 81; −3 is also a fourth root of 81, and the radical denotes 3.

The radicand may be a whole number or a fraction such as 18/25, and it is allowed to be negative.

Method

Simplifying a radical

Check that the thing exists before working on it. That order saves a page of algebra now and again.

1

Check the sign of the radicand against the index

Even index and a negative radicand means there is no real value, and that is the finished answer rather than a dead end. Odd index means there is always exactly one, and it carries the sign of the radicand.

2

Factor the radicand into primes

72 is 2³ · 3²; 54 is 3³ · 2. Guessing at a large square factor works sometimes and fails silently when it is not the largest, which leaves a half-simplified answer. The prime factorisation cannot leave one.

3

Take out complete groups of the index

A pair of equal primes under a square root sends one copy outside; a triple under a cube root sends one copy outside. Anything that cannot fill a group stays in.

4

Check what is left, and check the value

No prime under the sign should appear to the index or higher, and no fraction should be left under it. Then raise your answer to the index and confirm you get the radicand back.

Three radicals, one method

Each one is factorised first, and each answer is raised back to the index at the end.

1. √72 72 = 2³ · 3² = 2² · 3² · 2 complete pairs out: 2 and 3 left in: 2 √72 = 6√2 check: (6√2)² = 36 · 2 = 72 2. the 3rd root of −54 −54 = −(3³ · 2) complete triple out: −3 left in: 2 3rd root of −54 = −3 · (3rd root of 2) check: (−3)³ · 2 = −27 · 2 = −54 3. √(18/25) √(18/25) = √18 / √25 18 = 3² · 2, so √18 = 3√2; √25 = 5 √(18/25) = 3√2 / 5 check: (3√2 / 5)² = (9 · 2)/25 = 18/25

Each check raises the answer to the index and recovers the radicand. That establishes the value is a root of the radicand. For the two square roots it takes one more observation to know it is the principal root: 6√2 and 3√2 / 5 are both positive, and positivity is the second half of the definition.

Line 2 keeps the minus sign outside the radical throughout, which is the tidy way to handle an odd index over a negative radicand. Line 3 leaves no root below the bar because 25 is a perfect square; had the denominator been 24, the answer would need the rationalising step of course 5.

Common mistakes

1
Writing that the square root of 9 is plus or minus 3The symbol names one number. The ± is written by hand when you solve x² = 9, precisely because the radical would otherwise supply only the positive root. Losing that distinction loses a solution in course 6.
2
Splitting a root over a sum√(9 + 16) is √25 = 5, not 3 + 4. The product rule √(ab) = √a · √b is real and the sum version is not, which is what makes the false one look reasonable. Two numbers settle it in seconds.
3
Cancelling the square root of x squared to xIt is |x|. At x = −5 the left side is 5 and the right is −5. For an odd index the cancellation really is clean — the cube root of x³ is x — and that is exactly why the even case is forgotten.

Completion standard

✓
Finish when you can say whether a radical has a real value before you try to simplify it.

Given a radical, the first two things you should produce are the parity of the index and the sign of the radicand, and only then a factorisation. You should also be able to state, without hesitating, that √9 is 3 and that x² = 9 has two solutions, and say why those are not in conflict.

Note: Lesson 8 gives the radical a second notation: x^(1/n). That is not a new idea but a forced one — if the exponent laws of lesson 5 are to hold for fractional exponents, then (x^(1/2))² must be x¹, and x^(1/2) has no choice but to be the square root. Every restriction stated here, including the even-index one, travels with it.
