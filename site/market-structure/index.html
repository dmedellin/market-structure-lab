Market Structure

Market structure — simple, technical, and fits an interactive format.

Seven lessons that teach you to read a bare chart: where structure is, where it breaks, how to enter it, how to be wrong cheaply, and how to express the idea. Every lesson is one self-contained interactive page — no accounts, no tracking, nothing to install.

Start with lesson 01 See all seven lessons

The course

What this course is

One subject, taught in order. Market Structure takes the parts of technical trading that can actually be shown on a chart and turns each one into a page you can click through.

7 lessons · ~2 hr 40 min

What it teaches

Reading price without indicators: swing structure and breaks of structure, range boundaries and liquidity sweeps, higher-timeframe context, three entry models, structural invalidation and reward-to-risk, volume and relative strength, and finally how an options contract is chosen to express the idea.

Who it is for

Anyone who can open a chart and wants a vocabulary for what is on it. No prior technical analysis is assumed — lesson 01 starts from a bare candlestick chart — and no platform, account, or subscription is needed. The later lessons stay useful if you already trade and want a stricter definition of invalidation.

How to work through it

In order, 01 to 07. Each lesson assumes the one before it: entries (04) are placed on the pullbacks defined in 01–03, stops (05) are placed at the structural levels those lessons named, and contract selection (07) prices the plan built in 05. Take one per sitting; each ends with a way to test yourself.

Learning path

After the seven lessons

Market Structure is course 1 of the trading path. It teaches you to read the chart; course 2 turns that reading into a written, sized, and reviewed trade, course 3 takes the same plan into options, and course 4 adds indicators. The path runs to eight courses in all — the later ones are listed on the trading path page, and the ones that are not published yet are marked there. Take them in order: each one assumes the one before it.
